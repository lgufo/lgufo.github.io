Hardware–software co-optimization on Gemmini

Spring 2026 · ELEC 5140 Advanced Computer Architecture · with Puyang Zhang

A neural network spends its time on multiply-adds, and on moving the numbers those multiply-adds need. Gemmini generates a small accelerator next to a RISC-V core for that pattern. The core sends commands. A systolic array, a grid of tiny calculators that pass values to their neighbors, does the arithmetic. A scratchpad and an accumulator keep the current tile on the chip. A DMA engine carries tiles to and from main memory, and a TLB translates the addresses on the way.

Puyang Zhang and I ran MLP and CNN kernels on Gemmini’s simulator. My part was the hardware sweep: array size (8×8, 16×16, 32×32), on-chip memory, the TLB, how many memory requests could be in flight at once, and the reservation station, the queue of loads, stores, and executes waiting to run. Puyang’s part was the tiling, choosing loop tiles by hand so they fit the scratchpad and the accumulator. The useful experiments changed both.

Small kernels

The small MLP is two matrix multiplies, 16×64×128 and then 16×128×32. The small CNN takes a 13×13 image with 3 channels down to 7×7 with 16 channels. A wider array helps when the kernel is full of arithmetic. On the small CNN it barely did: moving from 8×8 to 16×16 dropped the total from 5,129 cycles to 4,827, while TLB wait stayed near 1,850 cycles and a full reservation station stayed near 4,600. The extra calculators had little new work.

Manual tiles, sized to the array and the on-chip memories, moved the totals more. On the 16×16 MLP, cycles fell from 3,985 to 2,207, about 1.8×. On the CNN with a larger TLB and more in-flight memory requests, they fell from 4,530 to 2,728, about 1.66×. “+TLB” below is that larger TLB and in-flight setting.

A heavier convolution

The larger CNN has a 28×28 output, 128 input channels, 128 output channels, and a 3×3 kernel. After the image is flattened into a matrix, the multiplication is 784×1152 by 1152×128. That shape has plenty of arithmetic, and it does not fit in the accumulator as one tile.

Growing the array under automatic tiling, from 16×16 to 32×32, cut cycles from 523,798 to 228,781, about 2.3×. Manual tiles on the 32×32 array cut that further to 130,877, about 1.7× against the automatic schedule on the same array. Together, against the 16×16 automatic baseline, the speedup is 4.0×. The same manual tiles helped less on the 16×16 array (523,798 to 468,703) than on the 32×32 array, which is the slide deck’s observation: the tile shape matters more once the array is wide enough to be left idle.

Where the cycles went

On the 32×32 array the execute time barely changes between the two schedules, about 111,000 cycles either way. What changes is the waiting. TLB wait falls from 86,842 cycles to 1,646. Cycles spent with the reservation station full fall from 219,468 to 121,554. The tile shape did not make the array faster at arithmetic. It stopped the array from stalling on address translation and on a full command queue.

Pushing the other knobs further stopped paying off once they were no longer the bottleneck. On this convolution, a 16×16 array with the default reservation station (load, store, and execute entries at 8/4/16) already sat at 523,798 cycles with automatic tiles and 468,703 with manual tiles, and a deeper queue did not move those totals. A larger TLB and more in-flight requests did help the small CNN, from 4,827 cycles to 4,530 under automatic tiling. Extra hardware paid off when the schedule could use it.
