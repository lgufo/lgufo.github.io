Newcomers on RedNote

Jan 2025 – Sep 2025 · With Kangyu Yuan, Hanfang Lyu, Ziqi Pan, Yuanhao Zhang, Junze Li, Bingcan Guo, Jiaxiong Hu, and Qingyu Guo · Prof. Xiaojuan Ma

In January 2025, after the U.S. government moved to ban TikTok, a large number of its users joined RedNote, a platform whose existing community is predominantly Chinese. Newcomers called themselves TikTok refugees. Prior studies of platform migration mostly concern newcomers and long-time users who already share a cultural context. This migration runs in the other direction: users from a Western platform entered a community based in China.

In the HKUST HCI Initiative we studied the start of that arrival: which posting practices supported the first contact between newcomers and existing users, and which problems appeared before either group had settled into a routine.

Data

We collected public posts under institutional review. Starting from English and Chinese hashtags related to the migration, we retrieved posts with MediaCrawler. The first query returned 1,143 posts. We then ranked frequent hashtags in that set, retained those that referred to the migration, and queried again. After removing duplicates and unrelated posts, 3,510 posts remained, all dated 13–16 January 2025. A post was retained if it was part of an exchange between newcomers and existing users, or if it discussed that exchange. Internet-protocol location placed about 47% of the posts in the United States and about 39% in China.

We analysed the posts by open coding, without a predefined codebook. The coding produced twelve strategies, grouped into five themes. A post need not use every strategy. A greeting, a pet photograph, or a request for local norms can each initiate an exchange on its own.

Theme Posting practice
Opening A greeting, a self-introduction, or a request for further interaction, often expressed only by a tag.
Orientation A signal of participation, commonly a pet photograph, and requests for or offers of community norms.
Conversation Exchange of everyday information, practical help, or a short video or image.
Reaction Description of the influx, and reports of personal response to it.
Reflection Commentary on the event, and posts that used it for advertising.

Findings

Posts that were polite and easy to interpret accounted for much of the successful first contact. Openings appeared in 48.8% of posts. Among posts whose authors were not writing in Chinese, 45.6% included a Chinese translation, sometimes with a note on a mistranslation. A frequent participation signal was what participants called an animal tax: a photograph of a pet, usually a cat. The image requires no shared language and carries no group affiliation. Newcomers also requested community norms, and existing users supplied them. The early record therefore shows mutual accommodation, where studies of other sudden influxes more often record norm violations and pushback from the existing community.

Continued participation is a separate problem. An opening exchange does not by itself become repeated interaction. The early set also included posts outside the platform’s rules, including screenshots of harassment in private messages, which argues for moderation at the start of the influx. Some existing users who were not following the migration still received a large volume of related recommendations. The design discussion in the paper concerns support for the initial cross-cultural exchange and preservation of the rest of the platform while that exchange is underway.
